🪨Caveman version

The whole memo in plain words. Full detail follows below.

What company do? Most AI computers use many small chips that keep passing information around. Cerebras makes one giant chip with very fast memory beside the workers. Less passing stuff around can mean much faster answers.

Why care? If an AI writes code or carries out many steps, waiting is expensive. OpenAI wants Cerebras speed. Other companies might pay for that speed too.

Where catch? Fastest car not always cheapest taxi fleet. Company still must build the data centers, keep machines busy, collect money, and pay for everything. A huge contract is a promise to deliver, not money already earned.

Money picture? Business growing fast. Still loses money. Raised a big pile of IPO cash. Also gives stock to workers and customers, so each existing share can own less of the company later.

Is $90 cheap because IPO was $185? No. Smaller price tag than before does not automatically mean good deal. Investors still paying a very large price for today's sales.

My call: Interesting giant brain chip. Risky stock. Watch November 12. Want proof of more delivered capacity, better real margins, and sensible cash use—not just bigger promises.

Executive summary

Verdict: Speculative / high-risk watchlist — genuine engineering, but not yet a proven bargain after the selloff. I would wait for the November 12 results and clearer deployment, dilution and cash-spending evidence before treating CBRS as a long-term investment rather than a venture-style bet.

The investment in one sentence: Cerebras may win a valuable slice of fast AI inference without replacing Nvidia, but its equity price already assumes a large business that has not yet been built.

  • The product is real, not just a presentation. Wafer-scale processors combine unusually large amounts of compute and fast memory. OpenAI independently confirms a 750 MW partnership. Independent research supports low-latency advantages, but not universal cost or power superiority. [1, 2, 5, 7, 8]
  • Cloud growth is strong; revenue quality needs care. Q2 GAAP cloud/services revenue grew 281% to $126.0M. Total GAAP revenue was $180.1M, versus $209.9M on management's adjusted “core” definition. Customer equity incentives reduce GAAP revenue; low-margin data-center reimbursements increase it. [2, 3]
  • The customers are concentrated, even as the names change. MBZUAI and G42 together generated 86% of 2025 revenue. Three customers represented 76% of Q2 2026 revenue. OpenAI contributed $56.8M in Q2 and dominates a significant portion of the $25.4B remaining performance obligations (RPO). [1, 2]
  • The balance sheet buys time, not automatic success. June unrestricted cash plus short-term investments was $7.92B; another $685M was restricted. H1 operating cash burn plus cash capex was about $596M. OpenAI's $918M outstanding loan and growing lease obligations matter. [2]
  • Cheap relative to the IPO is not cheap relative to sales. At $89.68, using all three share classes outstanding August 5, indicative equity value is about $21.3B and enterprise value about $14.3B using June financial balances. That is roughly 21x trailing GAAP sales, before a fully updated dilution and cash-spending adjustment. [2, 4; calculations below]
  • Near-term event risk is unusually concentrated. The prospectus's 180-day lockup endpoint calculates to Nov 9; market commentary also cites Nov 10 eligibility. Do not assume a precise new supply count or forced selling. Q3 results are confirmed for Nov 12 after the close, with the call at 5:00 PM EST. These events are still ahead as of this memo. [1, 6]

1. Identity, origins and history

Confirmed identity: CBRS is Cerebras Systems Inc., listed on the Nasdaq Global Select Market. This is an AI compute company, not a wireless-spectrum business. The final prospectus, Q2 10-Q and investor FAQ independently identify the ticker. [1, 2, 9]

The company's history page dates its founding effort to 2015, with Andrew Feldman, Gary Lauterbach, Michael James, Sean Lie and Jean-Philippe Fricker. Legal Delaware incorporation followed in April 2016. These dates describe different milestones, not necessarily a contradiction. The founding thesis was that AI was constrained by communication between processors and memory, and therefore needed a purpose-built architecture. [1, 10]

My assessment: The strongest historical evidence is repeated delivery of unusual hardware and actual revenue. The most important change for investors is not simply another faster chip: it is the move into a capital-intensive cloud business with multi-year delivery obligations.

2. Products explained without chip-industry jargon

Training versus inference

Training teaches a model by repeatedly changing its internal weights. Inference runs an already-trained model to produce answers. Cerebras can support both, and a model does not have to be trained on Cerebras to run inference on it. The current commercial emphasis is fast inference. Hardware-versus-cloud disclosures are not the same as training-versus-inference revenue; the filings do not provide a complete audited split by workload. [1, 2]

An inference request has two important stages. Prefill reads the prompt and builds working state; it often needs substantial matrix-compute throughput. Decode produces new tokens sequentially; moving the model's data fast enough can be the bottleneck. Cerebras's abundant on-chip SRAM particularly targets this second problem. A fast decode engine is not automatically the best engine for every other AI task. [7, 8, 11]

What wafer scale changes

Normally, a manufacturer prints many chips on a silicon wafer, cuts them apart, and reconnects them in servers. Cerebras keeps a very large connected area intact. Its WSE-3 combines approximately 4 trillion transistors, 900,000 AI-optimized cores and 44 GB of SRAM across 46,225 square millimeters on TSMC's 5nm process. It uses cross-reticle connections and redundant compute/routing resources so a defect does not automatically ruin the entire processor. [1]

Why that helps: on-chip communication is fast and avoids some external networking. Why it is not magic: SRAM is fast but takes substantial silicon area. Forty-four gigabytes is not enough to hold every large model and its long-context working memory in one wafer. Larger workloads still need multiple wafers, external infrastructure and communication. A quoted on-chip bandwidth cannot be treated as equivalent to the usable memory capacity or end-to-end speed of an entire GPU cluster. [1, 7, 8]

CS-3 versus the new CS-4

CS-3 houses one WSE-3. The CS-4 announced August 18 is a rack-scale design using three WSE-3 Turbo processors, not simply one new chip named “WSE-4.” Each Turbo retains 44 GB of SRAM and increases compute and bandwidth. [11]

These are vendor specifications, not measured investment returns. The sixfold rack-level compute increase includes both faster wafers and three wafers instead of one. It is not a sixfold same-chip or same-power gain. The release's “up to 10x throughput per watt” comparison is versus CS-3, while “up to 30x faster” is a per-user inference comparison versus GPU solutions under specified workloads. Those are different denominators. [11]

CS-4's modular “backpack” integrates direct liquid cooling, power conversion, I/O and control electronics. It supports Ethernet/RoCE and direct wafer links. The announcement said first shipments would begin that quarter. I have not independently verified the number shipped or production fleet economics as of Nov 8. [11]

Software and ease of adoption

The platform includes a PyTorch-oriented compiler, inference-serving software, cluster management and model engineering services. Cloud APIs reduce friction for customers consuming supported models. They do not make arbitrary CUDA software portable without work. Custom kernels, unusual operators, model architecture changes, numerical equivalence, compilation and production reliability still need validation. Independent experiments found real tooling limitations despite advertised framework support. [1, 7]

3. Manufacturing, memory and infrastructure constraints

Foundry concentration: the prospectus identifies TSMC as the foundry for the proprietary processor. Moving production is not like swapping a commodity supplier: the design, process, yield and packaging must work together. Taiwan disruption therefore remains a significant risk. [1]

Packaging and thermal execution: a large silicon device must survive assembly, mechanical expansion, power delivery and liquid cooling. Defect tolerance solves one yield problem; it does not abolish packaging defects, cooling failures, repair costs or field reliability requirements. The latest modular design may improve manufacturability, but the company has not publicly supplied enough independently audited yield, per-rack cost or failure-rate data here to quantify that advantage. [1, 11; judgment]

Different bottlenecks, not no bottlenecks: management says its architecture avoids HBM, CoWoS and 3nm fabrication constraints. That is helpful diversification from GPU supply chains. It still needs wafers, contract manufacturing, components, grid connections, cooling, commissioning and customers ready to accept capacity. The Aug 12 release named Flex, Sanmina and Rocket EMS manufacturing lines and said capacity would increase more than tenfold in 2026. This is a company plan, not a verified tenfold shipment outcome. [3]

Power booked is not power operating: management reported more than 600 MW live and under contract for delivery by end-2027. It did not say all 600 MW were already live. The OpenAI agreement calls for 750 MW deployed in tranches through 2028. These dates and definitions differ; subtracting one figure from the other does not establish a simple capacity shortfall. [2, 3, 5]

Transition costs are visible: the 10-Q reports $19.2M of H1 charges for excess, obsolete and scrapped inventory, primarily related to next-generation transition. That is concrete evidence that improving hardware can impair the economics of prior inventory. [2, Note 8]

4. How Cerebras earns money—and what counts as revenue

  1. Hardware: sells systems and associated equipment; revenue is recognized when control transfers, depending on shipment, delivery or acceptance terms. This can be lumpy.
  2. Dedicated cloud capacity: generally take-or-pay contracts. Customers pay for reserved capacity irrespective of utilization, provided Cerebras delivers its contractual service.
  3. On-demand cloud: pay-per-token or prepaid token bundles, plus training capacity for contracted periods. Easier to adopt, but usage and pricing are less predictable.
  4. Support, operations and custom modeling: multi-year support, management of customer clusters and AI engineering services.
  5. Data-center pass-through: reimbursement of certain costs incurred for specific customers. Recorded gross under the applicable arrangements, but carries very little gross profit. [2]

Important distinction: dedicated contracts move some utilization risk to customers, but not construction, delivery, service-level, counterparty, collection or renewal risk. Cloud revenue should not be valued automatically like high-margin subscription software.

5. Customers, contracts and concentration

G42 and MBZUAI: the old concentration did not disappear overnight

The prospectus states that G42 represented 85% of 2024 revenue. In 2025, MBZUAI represented 62% and G42 24%, a combined 86%. These are disclosed customer relationships, not assumptions based on geographic sales. The Q2 filing separately notes that Customers A and D are related parties to each other. I do not assign identities to every anonymized customer letter without a direct disclosure. [1, 2]

Q2 2026 customer shares were 34%, 32% and 10% for the three largest disclosed customers; the largest two therefore supplied 66% and the top three 76%. The largest accounts-receivable customer represented 57% of receivables at June 30. Dependence is broadening away from the original customer, but remains high. [2, Note 3]

OpenAI: a binding commitment, with financing and equity strings

The December 2025 Master Relationship Agreement covers 750 MW of committed inference capacity, delivered in tranches during 2026–2028, with service terms of three or four years, extendable by OpenAI to five years. OpenAI separately has an option for an additional 1.25 GW by end-2030. The optional capacity is not the same thing as the committed 750 MW and should not be presented as already booked deployment. OpenAI's own January 14 announcement confirms 750 MW and phased delivery through 2028. [2, 5]

Cerebras recognized $56.8M Q2 / $74.4M H1 revenue from this arrangement, net of specified warrant amortization. This is stronger evidence than a partnership press release, but far smaller than the multi-year opportunity. [2]

OpenAI also supplied approximately $1.005B of working-capital financing. June outstanding principal was $918.2M, after $86.3M of non-cash service credits; there had been no cash repayment. The note carries a stated 6% rate, subject to contractual waiver/deemed-payment provisions, and matures no later than Dec 31, 2032. Restricted proceeds fund permitted infrastructure uses. Acceleration provisions can matter if the relationship terminates under specified circumstances. [2, Note 10]

The OpenAI warrant covers up to 33.445M Class N shares at a nominal exercise price, with milestone conditions. A total of 10.034M shares vested by June and was exercised in July. Additional capacity-related tranches were not all considered probable of vesting as of June. This is meaningful dilution; it is not a standard cash purchase at the IPO price. [2, Notes 12 and 17]

RPO is not this year's sales

At June 30, RPO was $25.4B; the company says a significant amount relates to OpenAI. Expected recognition: 22% within 24 months to June 2028, 43% in months 25–48, and the remainder later. Applying those percentages gives approximately $5.6B and $10.9B, respectively—calculations, not new guidance. [2]

RPO includes eligible pass-through costs for certain committed-capacity arrangements already reflected in recognized leases; other pass-through costs are excluded where consideration remains outside company control. Therefore $25.4B is not pure high-margin chip/cloud revenue. It is also not cash on hand, guaranteed profit, or a market-cap floor. Customer-requested delivery timing can change recognition. [2]

AWS, AMD and other commercial relationships

  • AWS: a June global hardware leasing agreement for a joint solution. At June 30, commencement conditions had not been satisfied and no revenue had been recognized under that arrangement. AWS also received a warrant for up to 2.697M shares at $100, largely contingent on payment milestones. The Aug 12 release expected disaggregated inference on Bedrock in Q1 2027. That is prospective, not current recognized revenue. [2, 3]
  • AMD: partner as well as competitor. The Aug 12 release described disaggregated inference with AMD and expected production in Q4 2026. The complementary role is prefill on other compute, fast decode on Cerebras. [3, 11]
  • Broader cloud demand: the Q2 release named new capacity agreements with Cognition and Lovable and relationships including CrowdStrike, Block, Figma, AlphaSense and GSK. The filing does not disclose enough per-customer revenue to prove material diversification from these names alone. [3]
  • Oracle: the Oct 27 company IR news listing reports plans to bring Cerebras inference to OCI. Treat it as a distribution opportunity; no recognized Oracle revenue or signed dollar backlog is assigned in this memo. [12]

A less obvious constraint: the 10-Q warns that certain customer agreements, including the OpenAI MRA, restrict support, collaboration or sales to named competitors. That can make winning a large anchor customer simultaneously narrow other opportunities. [2, risk factors]

6. Leadership, execution and governance

Technical execution deserves credit

Andrew Feldman, CEO/president and co-founder, has led Cerebras since incorporation. Before this he was CEO of SeaMicro, which AMD acquired, then an AMD executive. Sean Lie, CTO/co-founder, has AMD and SeaMicro architecture experience. Dhiraj Mallick, COO, previously held senior AMD and Intel positions and has led engineering/operations at Cerebras. These are relevant systems-building backgrounds. [1, Management]

Other founders include chief system architect Jean-Philippe Fricker, advanced-technologies architect Michael James, and CTO emeritus Gary Lauterbach. The company's public history credits all five founders. [10]

Robert “Bob” Komin, CFO and treasurer, is listed in the prospectus as serving since March 2024. The company publicly announced his appointment in August 2024. His prior roles include Sunrun, Flurry and Ticketfly. The filing is the source for tenure; the later announcement should not be mistaken for the exact start date. [1, 13]

Material governance negatives should not be hidden

Historical legal record: the prospectus discloses that Feldman pled guilty in December 2007 to one count of circumventing accounting controls at Riverstone Networks, receiving three years' probation and a $5,000 fine. It separately describes a 2008 SEC settlement, without admitting or denying the allegations, requiring $289,507 plus interest and an injunction. This is a disclosed historical matter, not an allegation of current wrongdoing at Cerebras. It is nevertheless relevant when evaluating financial-reporting culture. [1, “Involvement in Certain Legal Proceedings,” pp. 151–152]

Current controls: June disclosure controls were not effective at reasonable assurance because of weaknesses in IT general controls, segregation of duties and accounting/finance staffing. Affected processes included revenue, inventory, data-center assets and equity administration. Management said the deficiencies had not caused a material financial-statement misstatement, but remediation was not yet established as complete. [2, Item 4]

Voting: A shares carry one vote, B shares 20, and N shares none. Economic rights are otherwise substantially aligned. The prospectus estimated B holders would control approximately 99.2% of votes immediately after the offering on its stated assumptions. That is an IPO-date estimate, not a current founder ownership percentage. There is also a classified board with staggered three-year terms. [1, 2]

Board: the May prospectus names Feldman plus Paul Auvil, Elena Donio, Lior Susan, Steve Vassallo and Eric Vishria, with the five non-executives deemed independent. The 2024 board announcement is not the right source for the current roster. [1]

Compensation and dilution: Q2 SBC was $377.0M, including $273.6M connected to satisfaction of IPO liquidity conditions for RSUs. It is misleading to annualize that catch-up expense as normal cash burn. It is equally misleading to erase equity cost altogether. New CEO/CTO performance awards cover 9M shares with market-cap milestones of $75B, $150B and $250B; the filing reports $531.2M aggregate grant-date fair value, recognized over derived service periods if service is provided. [2, Note 13]

My leadership verdict: strong evidence of engineering persistence and commercial ambition; mixed evidence on governance and reporting readiness. Independent audit expertise is a positive, but the control weaknesses and multi-class structure justify a higher required margin of safety.

7. Competition and the moat

Competitor / alternative Why it is formidable Where Cerebras may fit
Nvidia GPUs and CUDA ecosystem Software, kernels, networking, availability, broad model support and a very large installed base Ultra-low-latency decode where customers will pay for speed; complement rather than replace whole GPU fleets
Nvidia-associated Groq technology / LPU systems SRAM-heavy low-latency inference and an increasingly well-resourced commercial ecosystem Wafer-scale memory/locality advantages; must prove throughput and economics under real concurrency
AMD Instinct / Helios General GPU flexibility and growing hyperscaler support Partner for heterogeneous prefill/decode, while competing for infrastructure budgets
Google TPUs and other hyperscaler ASICs Captive demand, software integration, distribution and cost optimization Independent premium-inference specialist or partner, but not an automatic winner of internal workloads
AWS Trainium and custom silicon The platform owner controls procurement and can co-design workloads AWS partnership opens distribution but also creates dependence on a potential substitute
Other specialists, including SambaNova Different memory and dataflow tradeoffs Multiple architectures can win different parts of the latency/throughput frontier

The specific Groq/Nvidia competitive context is described in Aug 27 independent reporting; this memo does not assume Groq remains an entirely unchanged, isolated startup. [8]

What looks defensible: accumulated wafer-scale manufacturing know-how, fault tolerance, packaging/power/cooling integration, compiler expertise, a deployed fleet, and customer model co-design. Replicating the whole stack takes much more than designing a large die. [1; judgment]

What is not a sufficient moat: a single fastest-token chart, a large total-addressable-market estimate, or the fact that Nvidia is expensive. Rivals improve software and hardware; customers can route different requests to different systems. API compatibility helps Cerebras enter but also helps customers multi-source and switch.

CUDA switching barriers are real: inference behind a standard API has lower switching costs than porting an entire custom training stack. A scientist or AI lab with CUDA kernels, debugging practices, profiling tools and tested distributed infrastructure faces larger migration costs. Cerebras must win supported workloads on their actual requirements, not just claim “PyTorch works.” [1, 7; judgment]

8. Benchmarks: what has been demonstrated and what has not

Vendor claims

The prospectus cites up to 15x inference speed versus leading GPU solutions on selected open models. The CS-4 release claims over 4,400 tokens/second/user on GPT-OSS-120B and up to 30x GPU speed, while explicitly warning that model architecture, context, precision and serving configuration affect results. These are not universal ratios. [1, 11]

Independent evidence supports a niche, not a universal victory

The April 2026 xPU-athalon preprint from Harvard/Meta researchers evaluated CS-3 and several alternatives. In one small-model, low-batch test it measured Cerebras token latency at 22.89% of its H100 baseline. It found different winners as batch size, context and model size changed. It also found high CS-3 idle power, making utilization important. [7]

Limitations matter: this is a preprint, not an audit of today's entire commercial fleet; some comparisons use modeled scale-out or API access; comparator generations differ; it does not independently certify CS-4 economics. The paper itself contains inconsistent presentation of an energy formula versus accompanying prose, so I do not use its very large communication-energy ratios as an investment valuation input. Its directional findings are more robust than a universal headline multiplier.

Aug 27 reporting in The Register distinguishes batch-one “top speed” from economical multi-user serving. It explains how model weights and KV-cache capacity constrain concurrent long-context requests, and why combining GPU/other prefill engines with fast SRAM-based decode could improve the tradeoff. This supports the logic of the AMD/AWS roadmap while highlighting that integrated production economics still need proof. [8]

What I would require before accepting a lower-cost claim

  • Same model, model quality, numerical precision and accuracy requirements.
  • Same prompt/output lengths and context distribution.
  • Time to first token and inter-token latency at median and tail, not just one average.
  • Aggregate throughput at a fixed latency target and realistic concurrent demand.
  • Whole-system power, cooling overhead, utilization, capital cost, networking and support.
  • Availability, failure/retry rate, delivered cost per useful token and customer renewal behavior.

Bottom line: evidence of speed differentiation is credible. Evidence that Cerebras is always cheaper, more efficient, or a full Nvidia replacement is not established by the sources reviewed.

Why the profit numbers can mislead

FY2025's $237.8M GAAP profit included a $363.3M gain from extinguishment of a forward-contract liability. Operations still lost $145.9M; management's non-GAAP net result was a $75.7M loss. FY2024's large GAAP loss included a $401.3M forward-liability fair-value charge. Neither net-income headline is a clean measure of recurring chip/cloud economics. [1]

Q2 2026's $450.5M loss includes substantial IPO-related stock compensation. The earnings release's core net loss was $6.9M, but core operating loss was $33.6M and adjusted EBITDA was negative $53.1M. These measures have different definitions: adjusted EBITDA does not simply equal core operating profit plus depreciation because the adjustment sets differ, including warrant amortization. One cannot mix them into a single favorable profitability story. [3]

Margin bridge and business mix

Q2 gross profit was $25.6M versus $32.1M a year earlier despite higher sales. Core gross profit was $85.2M and core margin 40.6%, versus GAAP margin 14.2%. The adjustments include customer warrant amortization, SBC, IPO payroll tax and removal of pass-through economics. The huge gap is disclosed, not a reason to ignore GAAP. [3]

Cloud/services were about 70% of Q2 GAAP revenue, versus roughly 30% in FY2025 (calculations). As leased cloud infrastructure ramps, depreciation, under-utilization, startup costs and reimbursement mix can drive margins differently from a simple hardware seller.

Guidance is management's expectation, not a result

The Aug 12 outlook was core, not GAAP: Q3 revenue $214–216M, gross margin 38–40%, operating margin −25% to −23%; FY2026 revenue $880–890M, gross margin 41–43%, operating margin −19% to −17%. Management also said it planned to more than triple revenue in 2027. The latter is an ambition contingent on deployment, not a guaranteed 2027 outcome. Do not compare core guidance with GAAP historical revenue without a bridge. [3]

15. Decision framework and bottom line

Would make me more constructive: on-time customer-accepted capacity, growing GAAP gross profit with a comprehensible core bridge, diversification that is visible in revenue rather than logos, sustainable equity issuance, and a clear path from IPO-funded construction to positive cash returns.

Would make me step back: repeated delivery delays; rising RPO without collections; cash falling faster than deployment progress; continually expanding customer warrants; weak margins despite high utilization; or unresolved reporting problems becoming financial restatements.

Not a buy trigger by itself: down 51% from IPO, a positive single trading day, the size of the AI market, a giant backlog, a famous customer, or “30x faster.” None tells you the return on the price paid for each diluted share.

My conclusion: Cerebras has a credible chance to become an important specialist in fast inference. It does not need to defeat Nvidia everywhere. But shareholders need more than a technically successful company: they need delivered contracts, attractive economics and controlled dilution at a sensible price. At the verified $89.68 close, the right stance is high-interest watchlist, high skepticism on valuation, and patience for the Nov 12 evidence. This is a judgment about risk/reward, not a personalized instruction to trade.
