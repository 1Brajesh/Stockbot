🦴 Caveman version

Picking stock is not feeling. It is a checklist of 14 steps. Skip step = guessing.

Steps: know yourself and your rules. Check company grows and makes money. Explain it in one sentence. Check market is big. Know where every dollar of sales comes from. Look at 5+ years of history. Check cash and debt like a bank would. Check moat. Check boss is great with money. Write the full case for why you would hate it. Value it two different ways. Imagine bad, normal, good future. Decide how much to buy before buying. Re-check every 3 months.

No hype, no memes, no trends. Buy good companies. Buy a bit at a time, buy more when company is strong and price is down.

A methodical framework for evaluating every stock before you buy

"Be methodical. Avoid hype, memes, and trends. Buy good companies."

Stock selection is not a vibe check. It is a repeatable process. This guide walks through fourteen steps — from defining who you are as an investor to re-checking the thesis every quarter. Skip a step and you are guessing. Follow the sequence and you are investing with discipline.

The philosophy behind the process: treat investing as a discipline, not gambling. Use dollar-cost averaging. Add when fundamentals are strong and the price is down. Never buy based on a blue-sky story alone.

1. DEFINE YOUR INVESTING CONSTITUTION

Who You Are Before You Buy Anything

Decide who you are as an investor before buying anything. Are you long-term or short-term? What types of stocks do you buy? What are your goals, rules, and scoring system?

You do not invest without a clear game plan. Your constitution is the filter every stock must pass through. If you have not written it down, you do not have one yet.

2. DO A BASIC SANITY CHECK

Revenue, Profits, and Real Fundamentals

Confirm the company has revenue growth, has profits, and has real fundamentals. Never buy a stock based only on a blue-sky story.

If the business cannot show basic economic reality, stop here. No amount of storytelling fixes a company that does not earn money or grow revenue.

3. EXPLAIN THE BUSINESS IN ONE SENTENCE

The Clarity Test

If you cannot clearly explain what the company does and how it makes money, you do not buy the stock. Full stop.

One sentence. No jargon soup. If you need three paragraphs to explain the model, you do not understand it well enough to own it.

4. UNDERSTAND THE TOTAL ADDRESSABLE MARKET (TAM)

How Big Is the Opportunity?

Determine how big the market really is and whether the product is niche or scalable. No growth runway means no investment.

A great product in a tiny market caps your upside. A scalable product in a large market gives the business room to compound for years.

5. UNDERSTAND ALL REVENUE ENGINES

Where the Money Actually Comes From

Know exactly where revenue comes from: products, services, and segments. If you do not understand it, you do not invest.

Map every revenue line. Know which engines drive growth today and which could drive growth tomorrow. Surprises in revenue mix are how investors get blindsided.

6. REVIEW FINANCIAL HISTORY (5+ YEARS)

Consistency and Improvement Over Time

Study revenue trends, profitability trends, and margin trends over at least five years. You are looking for consistency and improvement — not one great quarter.

One-off spikes are not a business model. You want a track record that shows the company can execute through different environments.

7. ANALYZE THE BALANCE SHEET

The Loan Officer Mindset

Treat the balance sheet like a bank loan officer would. How much debt do they owe? How much cash do they have? What assets or collateral exist?

Balance sheet strength matters. A great income statement with a fragile balance sheet can still end badly when conditions tighten.

8. ASSESS THE MOAT

Defenses Against Competition

Identify defenses against competition: patents, network effects, customer lock-in, brand, or scale advantages. No moat means higher long-term risk.

Without a durable edge, today's winner becomes tomorrow's commodity. Moats do not have to be permanent, but they must be real and observable.

9. EVALUATE MANAGEMENT QUALITY

Elite Teams and Smart Capital Allocation

Only invest if management is elite, capital allocation is smart, and the execution track record is strong. Bad management kills good businesses.

Watch what they do with cash: reinvestment, buybacks, acquisitions, and compensation. Actions reveal priorities louder than earnings calls.

10. WRITE A FULL BEAR CASE

Pretend You Hate the Company

Pretend you hate the company. List all risks. Write the strongest possible bearish thesis. If you still want the stock afterward, proceed.

If you cannot write a convincing bear case, you have not done the work. Conviction without a bear case is just hope.

11. RUN VALUATION USING AT LEAST TWO MODELS

Never One Method Alone

Use multiple approaches: DCF, multiples (P/E, EV/EBIT, P/S, and others). Never rely on just one valuation method.

When two independent models agree you have a margin of safety, you have something worth sizing. When they disagree wildly, slow down.

12. BUILD SCENARIOS (BEAR / BASE / BULL)

Would You Still Own It in the Bear Case?

Ask whether you would still want this stock in the bear case. Only invest if the bear case is acceptable.

Scenario planning forces you to price in bad outcomes before they happen — not after your portfolio is already down 40%.

13. DECIDE PORTFOLIO SIZING

Position Size Is Risk Control

Determine in advance how much goes into this stock and how it fits with your S&P 500 allocation, bonds, cash, and other holdings. Position size is part of risk control.

Sizing is not an afterthought. Decide the weight before you click buy, not after the stock rips 30% and greed takes the wheel.

14. RE-EVALUATE EVERY QUARTER

Investing Is Ongoing Monitoring

Every quarter, recheck fundamentals, recheck the thesis, and confirm the stock still meets your criteria. Investing is ongoing monitoring, not set-and-forget.

The story can change. Your job is to notice when it does — and have the discipline to act when the thesis breaks.

15. CORE PHILOSOPHY BEHIND THE PROCESS

Discipline Over Gambling

Be methodical. Avoid hype, memes, and trends. Buy good companies. Use dollar-cost averaging and add when fundamentals are strong and the price is down. Treat investing as a discipline, not gambling.

This process exists so you can repeat good decisions — not so you can fall in love with a ticker and rationalize every red flag away.
