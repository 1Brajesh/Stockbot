Use free tools: Yahoo Finance, Seeking Alpha, SEC filings, company websites, Stock MVP, Notebook LM.

Write your rules first. Then check company with quick tests: sales growing? making money? more cash than debt? Fail = next stock.

Key numbers he wants: sales growth 10%+, profit margin 20%+, real cash coming in, more cash than debt, few people betting against it (short interest under 5%).

Explain the business in one sentence. Check it has a moat (hard for rivals to copy). Check the boss is a great doer. Then give company a 13-category score by hand, max 125. Under 75 = no. Read bad opinions about it on purpose. Check price last.

After buying, keep watching. Write down in advance what makes you sell.

Start small: try it on one company you know. Practice makes skill.

The Complete Step-by-Step Guide

"The goal of research isn't finding winners. It's avoiding losers."

Let me guess. You know you should be researching stocks before you buy them. But every time you open a financial statement, it looks like it was written in a foreign language. PE ratios, free cash flow, operating margins, beta. Nobody ever sat you down and explained what any of it means or what to actually do with it.

So most people skip the research. They buy whatever's hot on Reddit, whatever their brother-in-law mentioned at dinner, whatever some guy on YouTube screamed about. And then they wonder why their portfolio looks like a crime scene.

This guide fixes that. I'm going to walk you through the exact process I use to research a stock, from zero. What tools to open. What numbers to look at. What those numbers actually mean. And most importantly, when to walk away.

One thing before we start. The goal of research isn't finding winners. It's avoiding losers. Think of Howard Marks and amateur tennis. Just don't hit the ball in the net. The best investors in the world get it right about 60% of the time. Your job is to minimize mistakes, not swing for home runs every inning.

1. YOUR TOOLBOX

Free Tools Only

You don't need expensive software. Here's what I use:

Stock MVP (stock-mvp.com). My platform. It has the DCF calculator, reverse DCF, and insider buying data. This is where most of your research happens.

Yahoo Finance. Free. This is where you grab PE ratios, beta, EPS, shares outstanding, debt, and the financials. My go-to free resource.

Seeking Alpha. Reliable data for short interest and EBITDA. Also where you go to read bearish takes on a stock you like. More on why that matters later.

SEC filings (sec.gov). The company's actual reports. The 10-K is the annual report, audited and standardized. Like a McDonald's burger, they're all the same, so you always know where to look. The 10-Q is the quarterly version.

Company investor relations pages. Every public company is required by law to publish this stuff. All free.

Notebook LM (Google). My primary tool for analyzing earnings calls. No hallucination. Drop the transcript in and ask questions.

That's it. Don't pay for anything else.

2. WHAT THE NUMBERS MEAN

Plain English Definitions

Before the process, let's decode the language. These are the numbers I care about, in plain English.

Revenue growth. How fast sales are growing year over year. I want 10% minimum. 20%+ is ideal. Single digits and declining means I'm out.

Operating margin. Out of every dollar of sales, how much is left after running the business? Take income from operations and divide by revenue. I want 20% minimum. That's a binary test for me. 25%+ is excellent.

Free cash flow (FCF). How much actual money hits the bank account at the end of the day. This one matters more than profit. I want positive free cash flow for the past 4 to 6 quarters, no exceptions.

Cash is fact. Profit is an opinion of an accountant.

Cash vs. debt. Does the company have more cash than debt? It has to. Even by a dollar, if debt is bigger, no go. Debt is prison. Cash is freedom.

Short interest. The percentage of the stock that people are betting against. Below 5% is ideal. Above 10% and I'm not coming anywhere near that.

Forward PE. The stock price divided by what the company is expected to earn next year. Forward PE is life. Current PE is yesterday's news. One catch: only compare it to companies in the same industry. Comparing a bank's PE to a software company's PE tells you nothing.

The Rule of 40. My favorite 30-second test for growth companies. Add the revenue growth percentage to the free cash flow margin percentage. If the total is 40 or more, you've got a good company. Below 40, it's not. This works for software, subscriptions, fintech, and e-commerce. It doesn't work for banks, retail, or heavy manufacturing.

FCF margin. Free cash flow divided by revenue. Tells you how much of every sales dollar becomes real cash.

Scalability. Is revenue growing faster than expenses? I love companies where revenue grows 25% while expenses grow 13%. If expenses grow faster than revenue, the company fails the test. Binary.

Market cap. The total value of the company. Ignore the share price. A $5 stock isn't cheap and a $500 stock isn't expensive. The dollar share price is irrelevant. Only market cap matters.

Every step of the research process is an elimination round. Think about it like Survivor. Every time a company fails a step, it's out. You don't make excuses for it. You move on.

3. WRITE YOUR RULES BEFORE YOU LOOK AT ANYTHING

Step 1

Decide what kinds of businesses you want to own before you research a single stock. These rules are your constitution. You do not move the goalposts. If a stock doesn't fit, you never, ever shoehorn it into your portfolio.

4. FIGURE OUT WHERE THE WORLD IS GOING

Step 2

What does the future look like? AI, energy, robotics. Then look for the companies selling picks and shovels to those trends, not the lottery tickets.

5. THE SANITY CHECK

Step 3

Three questions. The company must pass at least 2 of 3, with a valid explanation for any failure:

  • Is revenue growing over the past few years?
  • Is it profitable?
  • Does it have more cash than debt?

If it fails this, you're done. Five minutes, next stock.

6. THE QUICK SCAN (5 MINUTES MAX)

Step 4

Open Stock MVP or Yahoo Finance and check the pre-screening list. The company must pass ALL of these:

  • Positive free cash flow for the past 4 to 6 quarters
  • More cash than debt for the past 4 to 6 quarters
  • Revenue growth of 10% or higher
  • An actual moat (covered in Check the Moat)
  • A CEO with a real track record
  • No major lawsuits or regulatory problems hanging over it

Any failure, it's out.

7. DESCRIBE THE BUSINESS IN ONE SENTENCE

Step 5

"Company provides X that helps Y achieve Z and charges this way." If you can't fill in that sentence, you don't understand the business, and you never evaluate numbers on a business you don't understand. Ask yourself three things. What pain are we curing? Who's in pain and why? Why will the customer come to us and not the competitor?

8. IS THE MARKET BIG ENOUGH?

Step 6

Avoid niche businesses in niche markets. Great tech with a tiny market is a bad investment. And don't trust lazy analyst reports throwing around random market size numbers.

9. RUN THE SCORECARD

Step 7

This one you run by hand, and that's a feature, not a bug. Scoring a company yourself forces you to actually look at the business instead of outsourcing your brain to a number on a screen.

Grab a sheet of paper or a spreadsheet. There are 13 categories. Each one gets a score of 0, 5, or 10. Maximum is 125. The hard numbers come straight from Yahoo Finance and Stock MVP. Use trailing twelve month data.

The starred categories are subjective. They can be massaged, which means they're where investors lie to themselves. Be brutally honest. If you're not sure whether a CEO is a superstar, they're not.

One note on recession proof. I don't believe there's any company that is totally 100% recession proof, so nobody gets a 10 in that category. Some pricing power earns a 5. That's the ceiling.

Add up all 13 scores. Here's how to read the result. And one more rule: when the market is frothy and risk is elevated, I raise my minimum to 100.

  • Below 75 — Absolute no-go. No matter how compelling the story, no matter what your uncle told you at the pub.
  • 75 to 100 — Investable, or watchlist material.
  • 100 to 125 — Once in a lifetime exceptional.

If you love a stock but it scores 75, the stock doesn't love you back.

Run the scorecard once when you first get interested in a company, then revisit it once a year. It's a screener, not a replacement for thinking. Think of it like a FIFA player card for the business. Deep research can override a borderline score, but nothing overrides a failing one.

10. CHECK THE MOAT

Step 8

A moat is what stops competitors from eating this company alive. Without a moat, revenue growth is irrelevant. There are six kinds: network effects, technology and patents, high switching costs, cost advantages, regulatory barriers, and brand power. The best companies have several at once.

My test: if a competitor threw 20 engineers at every one of this company's engineers and tried to copy the business, could they do it in a year? If the answer is no, there's a moat.

And check the direction, not just the existence. Is the moat getting stronger or weaker? Declining margins usually means the moat is eroding.

11. READ THE FINANCIALS (20 TO 30 MINUTES)

Step 9

Open the latest 10-Q. You're only reading three things.

Balance sheet: add up cash, cash equivalents, and marketable securities. Compare total assets to total liabilities. I want assets at least 2x liabilities.

Statement of operations: look at revenue, total operating expenses, and income from operations. Calculate the operating margin. Compare everything to the same quarter last year.

Cash flow statement: is the cash pile at the end of the period bigger than a year ago, without taking on new debt? That means the business generates real cash.

Skip the rest. You don't need it yet.

12. JUDGE THE CEO

Step 10

I will never invest in a company if I'm not fully amazed by the CEO. The single most important thing is track record of execution. Did they deliver in past roles? I don't care about excuses. At the end of the day you either did it or you didn't.

Bonus points if the CEO buys their own stock on the open market with their own money. And I want founder types who've been in the seat 10+ years, not a professional CEO brought in for four years. Remember: bozos hire bozos, superstars hire superstars.

13. TRY TO DESTROY YOUR OWN IDEA

Step 11

This is the step everyone skips and it's the one that saves you. Go to Seeking Alpha and read the bearish analysis on your stock. Write down 3 to 5 reasons your thesis might be wrong. Be as adversarial as possible to your own assessment. Then decide: can I live with that bear case or not?

14. VALUATION LAST, NOT FIRST

Step 12

Notice valuation comes at the end. Business quality first, price second. We buy a great company at a fair price, not a fair company at a great price. We're not dumpster diving.

Stock MVP has the DCF calculator. Run three scenarios: bull, base, and bear. The rule is simple. The company has to make sense as an investment over the next five years even in the bear case. Even the bear case has to be attractive.

15. KEEP WATCHING AFTER YOU BUY

Step 13

Research doesn't end at purchase. Trust but verify. Listen to every earnings call. Read the quarterly financials. Write down in advance what would make you sell, so you're never deciding in the heat of the moment.

16. THE AUTOMATIC DEAL-BREAKERS

Walk Away Immediately

Print this list. If a stock hits ANY of these, you walk away. No exceptions.

  • Market cap under $1 billion — Too much manipulation.
  • Short interest above 10% — I'm not coming anywhere near that.
  • Negative free cash flow — That means it's a startup, not an investment.
  • More debt than cash — Even by a dollar, no go. Debt is prison, cash is freedom.
  • Operating margin below 20% — Binary test.
  • Revenue growth below 10% and declining — Single digit growth is not for me.
  • The company's whole identity is its dividend — Heavy dividend payers are out.
  • Missed earnings in 2 or more of last 4 quarters — Management is probably full of it.
  • No moat — Without a moat, revenue growth is irrelevant.
  • Expenses growing faster than revenue — Fails scalability. Binary.
  • Seasonal business — I want sales in summer, winter, spring, and autumn.
  • Under 10 years old, from founding — Untested.
  • Scorecard below 75 — Absolute no-go.

And one language red flag: whenever you see "Adjusted EBITDA" in a company's reporting, run like the plague.

17. WHERE TO START THIS WEEK

Homework

Don't try to do all of this at once. Here's your homework.

First, pick one company you already know and use. Peter Lynch's rule, invest in what you know. Second, run it through the Sanity Check. Just those three questions. Third, pull it up on Yahoo Finance and find the revenue growth, the operating margin, and the cash vs. debt. Fourth, try to write the one-sentence business description.

That's it. Four small reps. You're not buying anything. You're learning to read. Do that with two or three companies and the numbers will start to feel familiar. Do it with ten and you'll wonder why this ever felt confusing.

One warning before you go. Reading this guide doesn't make you a stock picker any more than reading a cookbook makes you a chef. The skill comes from reps. So do the reps. Slowly. And never, ever skip steps because a story sounds exciting. The stocks that cost people the most money always come with the best stories.

The fact that you read this far instead of just buying whatever's hot on Reddit puts you ahead of 90% of investors. The system does the heavy lifting. Your job is to follow it and not skip steps.

Consistency. Discipline. Patience. That's the whole game.
