The Blueprint for Building Your Portfolio from Scratch

"Save more money, buy better companies, and stay invested for longer. This formula is undefeated."

Your portfolio is the most important financial structure you'll ever build. More important than any single stock pick. More important than any earnings call, any market prediction, any hot tip from your cousin. Get the architecture right, and the rest falls into place. Get it wrong, and no amount of brilliant stock picking will save you.

This guide gives you the complete blueprint. Every allocation table. Every rule. Every threshold. Whether you're building from scratch or redesigning a portfolio that looks like a yard sale, this is your architectural plan.

1. THE BIG IDEA

Why Architecture Beats Stock Picking

Here's something that might surprise you. Portfolio management is responsible for 50% of your portfolio's performance. Not stock selection. Not timing. Not reading tea leaves about what the Fed is going to do next Tuesday. Half of the game is how you structure your portfolio before you buy a single share of anything.

Most people get this completely backwards. They spend 95% of their energy hunting for the next hot stock and 5% thinking about how their portfolio actually fits together. Then they wonder why they're stressed, underperforming, and losing sleep. The portfolio looks like someone threw darts at a stock screener while blindfolded.

90% of this game is understanding who you are before you make a decision to buy or sell or hold. What's your timeline? How far are you from retirement? Can you stomach a 50% drop without panic-selling everything? All of these answers have to come BEFORE you open a brokerage account.

"Position sizing and allocation must be predetermined BEFORE evaluating any specific stock. All these factors have to be decided before you get to that point. Because if you get to that point and start making rules, you are already in love with the stock."

This guide gives you the architecture. Think of it like building a house. You don't start by picking the couch. You start with the foundation, the load-bearing walls, the structural framework. Then you furnish it. Your foundation is the S&P 500. Your load-bearing walls are the asset class mix. Your furniture is the individual stocks. In that order. Always.

2. THE FOUNDATION

Why Every Portfolio Starts with the S&P 500

Every portfolio MUST have a significant S&P 500 allocation. Not optional. Not "nice to have." Mandatory. This is a non-negotiable rule in my system.

The S&P 500 serves a dual purpose that most people don't fully appreciate. It protects your assets AND it protects your brain. When half my portfolio is flat over a bad week, it's a lot easier to stay calm, stay logical, stay relaxed. It protects you from yourself.

Look at the data. 95% of all 10-year periods in S&P history are positive. 100% of 20-year periods are positive. After 15 years, 95% of professional money managers lose to the S&P 500. This thing goes up no matter what. It's like the Pittsburgh Steelers defense from the seventies. It'll bend, but it will never, ever break.

The S&P 500 has a built-in mechanism that removes losers and keeps winners, giving you automatic diversification and downside protection without you lifting a finger. It's a cheat code.

But here's the key distinction. The S&P is protection, not growth. 40 to 50% of your portfolio goes there to protect your rear end. You're not supposed to get rich off the S&P 500. You get rich off your individual stock picks. The S&P is the safety net that lets you swing for the fences with the other half.

S&P 500 vs. NASDAQ as your anchor: I prefer the S&P 500. The NASDAQ drops 80% in tech-driven corrections like the year 2000, compared to about 50% for the S&P. I use the S&P as a psychological tool to protect myself from seeing 80% drops. The S&P is the slow boat, one of those big boats where you don't feel the turbulence. The NASDAQ is a speedboat. Faster, but bumpy ride.

My preferred S&P 500 vehicle: VOO (Vanguard).

FXAIX is also acceptable. VTI (Vanguard Total US Market) works too, though I personally prefer the S&P 500 concentration. I like the big winners. You don't drag a lot of trash with you. Any reputable ETF tracking a broad market index works. Whether it's Vanguard, Fidelity, Invesco, it doesn't matter long term. Just look at fees. For the defensive anchor of your portfolio, don't use growth-oriented ETFs like VUG. I'd rather have the defensive aspects of the S&P 500. For growth, I have my individual picks.

For 90% of investors, the absolute best strategy is buy the S&P 500 or Berkshire Hathaway and do nothing else whatsoever. Not pay academies. Not watch CNBC. That's the best absolutely hands-down strategy. If you can't commit to the work required for individual stock picking, go 100% S&P 500 and sleep like a baby.

3. THE CORE-SATELLITE MODEL

My recommended allocation for most investors: approximately 50% broad market ETF (S&P 500) plus approximately 50% individual high-quality stocks. This blend gives you both the safety net and the upside exposure of picking exciting stocks that might go to the moon.

The core is your S&P 500 ETF. Steady, reliable, boring. Exactly what you want from a foundation. The satellites are your individual stocks orbiting around it. These are where you hunt for multibaggers.

The individual stock side must justify displacing the index. Each individual stock must offer 5 to 10x potential to justify taking space away from the S&P 500. Whenever you find your multibagger in your head, bet on that. Make sure half is in the index and the other half is on your multibaggers. If a stock only offers 2x upside, it doesn't justify displacing a potential 5 to 10x play.

For more cautious investors (or anyone rolling over a retirement account): A 75% S&P 500 / 25% individual stocks split is excellent. If someone comes to me and says "I want to start with 75% in the index and 25% in 5 to 10 quality names, DCA the whole way," that's music to my ears. That tells me you get it.

A note about my personal portfolio. I run approximately 40% Palantir, 40% Tesla, and 20% S&P 500 (VOO). I'm telling you this for transparency, NOT as a recommendation. I wouldn't recommend my own style. Two stocks and the S&P 500 is a crazy allocation that makes no sense unless you have hyper conviction built from years of deep research. Normal investors should not try to replicate this. I'm not a good example here. Always follow the 50/50 or more conservative splits.

4. THE THREE REQUIRED ASSET CLASSES

A healthy portfolio has three asset classes: individual stocks, bonds, and a large market ETF (S&P 500). That's it. I personally only invest in two asset classes: stocks and bonds. I don't do options. I don't do commodities. I don't do crypto as a core holding. Keep it simple.

Each asset class has a job. The S&P 500 is your anchor. Individual stocks are your growth engine. Bonds are your insurance policy. You need all three working together.

The exact percentages in each asset class shift based on one variable that matters more than any other: your distance from retirement. Not your age. Your distance from retirement. Maybe you want to retire at 50. Maybe you want to work until 70. The number that matters is how many years until you need the money.

5. BOND ALLOCATION BY DISTANCE FROM RETIREMENT

There must be bonds in every portfolio, even if you're 25 years old. The importance and role of bonds isn't to beat your portfolio. Bonds won't beat the S&P 500. But they provide very valuable insurance. Bonds aren't just risk management for your psychological sanity. They're also there to give you dry powder to buy the dip when it comes.

You have to have defense to win championships. Bonds are defense.

Here's how I think about bond allocation based on distance from retirement. Remember, it's not about being 20 or 30 or 40. It's distance from retirement. People always ask, "how old are you?" No. The question is "when do you want to retire?"

I'm giving you the benchmark. You're a big boy or girl. You can make your own decisions. But notice the key shift at the 15-year mark: the S&P 500 becomes dominant at 60%, and high-growth exposure gets cut significantly. It's not ageism. It's just math.

The Rule of 110

Here's a simple baseline to start from. 110 minus your age equals the percentage you keep in equities. The remainder goes to bonds and safer assets. Age 30? That's 80% stocks, 20% bonds. Age 40? 70% stocks, 30% bonds. Age 60? 50% stocks, 50% bonds. Don't go crazy with it. It's just a baseline, a starting point, not a commandment.

Why Bonds Actually Matter: The Performance Data

If you take the 20-year period from 2000 to 2020, the classical 60/40 portfolio (60% stocks, 40% bonds) smashed the 100% stock guys. Here's the downside protection data from every major crash:

CrisisS&P 500 Drop60/40 Portfolio Drop

You're literally cutting your losses in half. Yes, 100% stocks will never underperform 60/40 over a 30-year horizon. But not everybody has 30 years. And not everybody can sit still through a 51% drawdown without doing something stupid.

Which Bond Vehicles to Use

I recommend buying bond ETFs over individual bonds for retail investors. Individual bonds need large upfront cash, have high transactional fees, and you can't diversify easily. Here are the ones I endorse:

ETFDescription
BND (Vanguard)Total bond market. The "S&P 500 of the bond market." 0.03% fee.
AGG (iShares)Total bond market alternative.
SWAGXTotal US bond market.
GOVTPure US Treasuries. No credit risk, no liquidity risk.

Bond tier ranking, in order: Tier 1 (best) is US Treasuries. No credit risk, highly liquid, the gold standard. Tier 2 (acceptable) is investment-grade corporate bonds at around 5% yield and municipal bonds, which are tax-free and great for high-bracket investors. Tier 3 is junk bonds. Absolute trash. They behave like stocks with high default risk. Never touch them.

Timing nuance: The best time to add bonds is during a hot market, when you can load up pretty cheaply while equities are running. Bonds can also be played offensively during rate-cut cycles: buy high-coupon bonds before cuts, and as new bonds issue at lower rates, your older bonds become more valuable.

6. THE THREE-FUND LOW-MAINTENANCE PORTFOLIO

This is my recommended portfolio structure for 85% of people. Busy individuals who don't want to pick individual stocks. People with demanding jobs, families, lives. If that's you, this is all you need.

Three components. That's it.

The percentages shift by your distance from retirement using the exact same bond allocation table from the previous section. If you're 35+ years from retirement, it's 10% bonds, 45% S&P, 45% Nasdaq. If you're 15 years out, it's 20% bonds, 60% S&P, 20% Nasdaq. Simple. Clean. Effective.

Set it up. Automate the contributions. Check it once per quarter. That's it. This approach will beat 90% of professional money managers over 15 years. The data is overwhelming on this point.

If you lose conviction or interest in individual stock picking, sell all of them, put your money in the S&P 500 and forget about it for the next five years. There is no shame in this. It's actually the statistically superior move for most people.

7. HOW MANY STOCKS?

The Position Count Rules

10 individual stocks is the absolute maximum. If you want a sustainable portfolio for a long, long time, you probably don't want to go over 10 because it's very hard to manage a 10-company portfolio if you're actually doing it the right way. Every stock you own requires quarterly earnings review, balance sheet analysis, competitive landscape monitoring, and management tracking. Every single quarter. That's a lot of work.

3 to 5 stocks is the ideal range for active stock pickers. Don't have a lot of stocks because you have to do all this work for all your stocks at all times. It's very, very hard when you have more than 3, 4, 5 stocks. I personally hold only 2 individual stocks because otherwise it becomes a hassle.

The Diworsification Warning

I've seen people with like a hundred different stocks in their portfolio because they've "diversified" themselves to death. Don't do it. It's stupid. More than 50 stocks turns your portfolio into an inferior index fund. You're paying individual stock commissions and doing individual stock research to build something that underperforms a single VOO purchase. That's insanity.

Your diversification comes from the index, not from adding more stocks. I get my diversification from my S&P 500 holding. I won't buy more companies just for the sake of diversifying my portfolio. The index self-updates. Less work, less stress, less probability of mistakes.

If your portfolio has 100% of its individual stocks in one sector (all tech, for example), it's going to swing very violently in one direction. That's not diversification. That's a concentrated bet with extra steps. Even with an S&P 500 core, make sure your individual picks aren't all in the same sector.

8. THE INVESTABLE CASH SPLIT

Deploy vs. Dry Powder

Of the money you've allocated to the stock market each month, here's the split: approximately 50% gets deployed into your portfolio. Approximately 50% stays as dry powder in your "DCA bank."

In good times, you deploy 50% into your portfolio and save 50%. In bad times, you deploy 100%, using the regular 50% plus your accumulated dry powder. The system is built on the fact that you've always invested 50%. In bad markets, you deploy 100% until it goes good again.

Your cash position is always a SIDE EFFECT of this system, never a deliberate macro call. I never have a cash position that is a set percentage of my portfolio. I never say "I want to be in 30% cash because I think the market is about to dip." That's timing the market, and I don't do that.

Where the dry powder sits: When I say cash, I usually mean three-month US Treasury bills paying a few percent annually. Not cash in a mattress. Not in a regular savings account earning nothing. Your dry powder should be working for you in short-term Treasuries or a money market account while it waits.

"Never go 100% cash. There's almost never a time where I'd say sell everything or go 100% cash. Even at peak euphoria, stay at least two-thirds invested."

Apple, Google, Amazon, they're all multinational corporations with massive exposure to the entire global market. You get all of that non-US exposure through US companies with much less risk. Geographical diversification is foolish because the US economy over the past 100 years has not only overperformed but smashed all other markets. Additional Portfolio Options

The Cockroach Portfolio (for a portion of your portfolio): Allocate 10 to 20% of your total portfolio to a defensive four-way split: Cash, Bonds, Gold, and Defensive Stocks (consumer staples, healthcare, telecom). The worst year ever for this structure was about -8%. The best was about +14%. Average annualized return is around 5.5%. In a crash, you're looking at about a 20% drop when the S&P drops 50%. Build this through new DCA contributions, not by selling existing holdings.

Gold: A small allocation is acceptable as insurance. I'm personally not a fan. Too volatile, not predictable. But a small insurance allocation of 2.5% to 7.5% of your portfolio is reasonable.

Bitcoin: Bullish long-term, but maximum 3 to 5% allocation. It has no place in a defensive portfolio. Too volatile. And 100% crypto is a lottery ticket, not investing.

10. BEFORE YOU BUILD

The Capital Allocation Waterfall

Before you invest a single dollar, every dollar of free cash flow must pass through these stops IN ORDER. You do not advance to the next step until the current step is complete. Not a single dollar passes through stop number one before it fills up.

STOP 1: Emergency Fund. 6 months of living expenses (minimum 3 months to begin investing, build to 6 while investing, 12-18 months in frothy/uncertain markets). Sits in liquid money market accounts or high-yield savings. NOT in stocks. This is the most important thing you have to do before anything.

STOP 2: Pay Off Expensive Debt. Anything above 7-8% interest, primarily credit card debt. You do NOT need to be 100% debt-free before investing. You just need a realistic plan to eliminate high-interest debt within about 6 months.

STOP 3: Tax-Advantaged Accounts. Max out your 401(k) AND Roth IRA. Do both. Max out both. Employer match is the number one priority. The government is literally giving you tax-free money.

STOP 4: Targeted Savings. Situation-dependent. College funds, HSAs for healthcare. Varies by age and life stage.

STOP 5: Stock Market. Then, and only then, at this stage we start talking about stocks and ETFs.

STOP 6: All Remaining Debt. Pay off ALL remaining personal debt before diversifying beyond stocks. No more mortgage, no student loans, no car loans. 100% debt free. Business debt is separate.

STOP 7: Real Estate. Only after all personal debt is cleared. Three vehicles: single-family rentals, REITs, or syndication/multifamily groups.

Hard boundary: if you have no discretionary income after paying bills, you should not be investing. Focus on generating more income, getting education, improving skills, building additional income streams, side hustles. The stock market will still be there when you're ready.

11. THE SEVEN DEADLIEST PORTFOLIO CONSTRUCTION MISTAKES

Mistake #1: Skipping the Foundation

Jumping straight to individual stock picks without an S&P 500 core. You're building a house without a foundation. When the earthquake hits (and it always hits), the whole thing collapses.

Mistake #2: Diworsification

Owning 50, 80, 100 stocks because someone told you diversification is good. You've built an index fund with higher fees and worse performance. Pick 3 to 5 great companies and let the S&P 500 handle diversification.

Mistake #3: No Bonds at Any Age

Even at 25, you need bonds. 10% minimum. They're not there to make you money. They're there so you have dry powder to buy when the market crashes 40%, and to cut your portfolio's losses in half during the worst periods.

Mistake #4: Investing Before Completing the Waterfall

No emergency fund? Credit card debt at 22% interest? You have no business buying stocks. If the market pulls back, the last thing you want to be doing is selling your portfolio to pay rent, getting pennies on the dollar.

Mistake #5: Mislabeled Diversification

Owning five different tech stocks and calling it diversified. If all your stocks behave in a similar pattern, you have no understanding of risk management. Mislabeled diversification is as dangerous as no diversification. When one drops, they all drop.

Mistake #6: Overmanaging

Vanguard studied millionaire portfolios. They traded only 8% of assets per year. 82% US stocks. 95% index funds. Only 1% sold during the COVID 2020 crash. People start tinkering with their portfolio to death. Selling, adjusting. Every time it has a reasonable explanation, but the cacophony of activity creates a huge problem. Don't mess with compound interest. It always has a story that doesn't have a good ending.

Mistake #7: Building Without a Plan

No target allocation. No DCA schedule. No pre-determined position sizes. Just buying whatever feels exciting in the moment. That's not investing. That's gambling with a brokerage account.

12. THE TOM NASH TAKE

Portfolio construction is not glamorous. It's not exciting. Nobody's making YouTube videos about asset allocation tables and bond ETF selection. But this is where the game is won or lost.

The formula has never changed. Save more money, buy better companies, and stay invested for longer. This formula is undefeated.

Your portfolio structure is like a fingerprint. It should reflect YOUR distance from retirement, YOUR risk tolerance, YOUR life situation. But the principles are universal. S&P 500 as the anchor. Bonds as insurance. Individual stocks only if you'll do the work. Position sizes that let you sleep at night. A DCA system that removes emotion from the equation.

Build the architecture right, and the furniture takes care of itself.

Boring means money in the stock market. We love boring.

Now go build your portfolio.

13. QUICK REFERENCE

Portfolio Construction Cheat Sheet

  • S&P 500 allocation: Mandatory. ~50% of portfolio for most investors. 75% for cautious profiles.
  • Individual stocks: ~50% of portfolio (25% for cautious). Each must offer 5-10x potential.
  • Bond allocation: Minimum 10% at any age. 50%+ near retirement. Use BND, AGG, SWAGX, or GOVT.
  • Position count: 3-5 ideal. 10 absolute maximum. More than 50 = inferior index fund.
  • Investable cash split: 50% deployed / 50% dry powder in good times. 100% deployed in bad times.
  • Dry powder vehicle: 3-month US Treasury bills or money market. Never cash in a mattress.
  • Three-fund portfolio: VOO + QQQM + SWAGX. Adjust % by distance from retirement.
  • Rule of 110: 110 minus your age = equity %. Remainder in bonds/safer assets. Baseline only.
  • Speculative plays: Never more than 5% of portfolio. At any age.
  • S&P 500 ETF: VOO preferred. FXAIX, VTI also acceptable.
  • Junk bonds: Never. Absolute trash. Behave like stocks with high default risk.
  • International stocks: Not recommended. S&P 500 companies already provide global exposure.
  • Minimum time horizon: 10 years for stock investing. Under 5 years = bonds/savings only.
  • Rebalancing method: Through purchases, not sales. Buy more of what's underweight.
  • Investment target: 15-20% of gross income. Start at 5% if needed and ramp up.

Rebalancing Rules

I don't sell to rebalance for tax reasons. If one stock is up and another isn't, I buy more of the laggard until it catches up. DCA allocation shifts dynamically. I don't put the same amount every month. It's always based on where the market is. In elevated-risk markets, maintain position sizes almost religiously. If a stock position grows 10% above its intended allocation, trim it back immediately.

Key Principles to Remember

  • Stock selection is personal, not universal. There is no "five best stocks in the market." Pick companies where you can analyze the business.
  • Pick companies immune to political chaos. Administrations change. Presidents change. Your investments must be weatherproof.
  • Compound interest is the biggest cheat code in investing. Trading in and out destroys it.
  • Minimum 10-year time horizon for stock investing. If the money is needed in under 5 years, it doesn't belong in stocks.
  • The portfolio must be overwhelmingly filled with quality before the never-sell philosophy applies.
  • If you can't commit to the research, go 100% S&P 500. Zero shame.

Stockbot's thoughts

Stock Bot's own commentary. This section is not part of Tom Nash's text.

What the guide gets right

  • A simple, sensible order of operations: emergency fund, high-interest debt and tax-advantaged accounts come before stocks.
  • It is realistic about the time cost of owning individual stocks.
  • It warns against over-trading and fake diversification, which are the mistakes that hurt most retail investors.

Cautions

  • Several statistics are the author's claims without sources: the 50% figure, and the 60/40 drawdown table.
  • "100% of 20-year periods are positive" depends on the dates chosen. It describes the past and is not a guarantee.
  • The 50% S&P / 50% "5–10x stocks" rule assumes you can reliably find multibaggers. Most people cannot.
  • "Never sell" and "never go below two-thirds invested" are rules of thumb that are not safe for everyone.
  • The Rule of 110 and the allocation table are simplistic, and the "within 5 years" row gives no figure for the remainder.
  • The US-centric models are a bet that US outperformance persists.
  • The author's own 40% Palantir / 40% Tesla portfolio contradicts his diversification and sector rules. He admits this himself.
  • A Nasdaq "growth" bucket plus single stocks can overlap heavily in the same big tech names.
  • This is general education, not personalized advice.
