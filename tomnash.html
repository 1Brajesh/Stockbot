Portfolio Construction 101

Digest of a guide by Tom Nash — summarized, not verbatim

🦴 Caveman version

Build house before couch. Foundation = big index fund (S&P 500, like VOO). Half of money in it, half in few great stocks that might go 5-10x.

Some bonds always (even young). Bonds are safety, and they give you cash to buy when market crash.

Few stocks only: 3-5 good, 10 max, because each one need homework every 3 months. Too many stocks = bad index fund. Do not pile all stocks in same sector.

Before buy any stock: have emergency money (6 months), kill big-interest debt, use 401k and Roth.

Put money in steadily, half now, half saved. When market crash, spend saved half too.

Don't mess with it. Don't trade a lot. Keep 10+ years. If no time for homework, put everything in S&P 500 and sleep.

Save more, buy good stuff, wait long.

1. The big idea: structure beats picking

  • The author claims portfolio management accounts for about 50% of performance, more than stock selection or timing. (His claim, unsourced.)
  • Decide allocation and position sizes before evaluating any stock. Once you are looking at a stock you like, you tend to bend the rules.
  • Know your timeline and whether you can stomach a 50% drop without panic-selling, before opening a brokerage account.
  • The house analogy: the S&P 500 is the foundation, the asset-class mix is the load-bearing walls, and individual stocks are the furniture.

2. The foundation: the S&P 500

  • An S&P 500 allocation is mandatory, roughly 40–50% of the portfolio. Its job is protection, not growth: it keeps you calm when the rest swings.
  • Claims quoted by the author: 95% of 10-year periods are positive, 100% of 20-year periods are positive, and about 95% of professional managers lose to the S&P 500 over 15 years.
  • He prefers the S&P 500 over the Nasdaq as the anchor, noting the Nasdaq fell about 80% in 2000 versus about 50% for the S&P.
  • Vehicles: VOO is preferred; FXAIX and VTI are acceptable.
  • For about 90% of investors his advice is to buy the S&P 500 (or Berkshire) and do nothing else.

3. Core-satellite model

  • Standard split: about 50% S&P 500 ETF (core) and about 50% individual stocks (satellites).
  • Cautious investors or rollover money: 75% S&P / 25% stocks, with 5–10 names, built through dollar-cost averaging (DCA).
  • Each stock must offer 5–10x potential to deserve space over the index.
  • Disclosure: the author's own portfolio is about 40% Palantir, 40% Tesla and 20% VOO, shown "for transparency, not recommendation." He says normal investors should not copy it.

4. The three asset classes

Bonds Insurance, and cash to buy dips BND, AGG, SWAGX, GOVT

He avoids options, commodities and crypto as core holdings.

6. The three-fund low-maintenance portfolio

For roughly 85% of people: VOO + QQQM + SWAGX (S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, and a bond fund), using the percentages from the retirement-distance table above. Automate the contributions and check quarterly.

7. How many stocks?

  • 3–5 is ideal and 10 is the maximum. Each holding needs a fresh review roughly every quarter, so more names means more homework.
  • Above about 50 positions you own an inferior index fund.
  • Avoid putting all picks in one sector.

8. Investable cash split: deploy vs. dry powder

  • Deploy about 50% of each month's investable money and keep about 50% as dry powder in 3-month T-bills or a money market fund.
  • In bad markets, deploy 100%, including the saved half.
  • Cash is a side effect of the system, not a macro call. Never go 100% cash, and at peak euphoria stay at least two-thirds invested.

Cockroach portfolio: 10–20% of the total in cash, bonds, gold and defensive stocks, split four ways. Gold at 2.5–7.5% is optional, and Bitcoin is capped at 3–5%.

10. The capital waterfall: do these in order

  1. Emergency fund: 3 months to start, 6 months as the target, 12–18 months in uncertain markets.
  2. Pay off debt costing more than about 7–8%.
  3. 401(k) (capture the employer match first) and Roth IRA.
  4. Targeted savings such as college and an HSA.
  5. Stock market.
  6. Pay off all remaining personal debt before diversifying beyond stocks.
  7. Real estate.

If you have no discretionary income, don't invest yet.

11. The seven deadliest mistakes

  1. Skipping the S&P 500 foundation.
  2. Diworsification: owning too many stocks.
  3. Holding no bonds.
  4. Investing before completing the waterfall.
  5. Mislabeled diversification (many names, one sector or theme).
  6. Overmanaging. He cites a Vanguard study of millionaire accounts: about 8% of assets traded per year, and only 1% sold in the 2020 crash.
  7. Having no plan.

12. Cheat sheet

Rule Number

The guide ends with a fill-in-the-blanks template covering your profile, waterfall check, asset allocation, stock positions, DCA system, bond detail and review schedule. If you can't commit to the research, he says to go 100% S&P 500 with no shame.

Stockbot's thoughts

What the guide gets right

  • A simple, sensible order of operations: emergency fund, high-interest debt and tax-advantaged accounts come before stocks.
  • It is realistic about the time cost of owning individual stocks.
  • It warns against over-trading and fake diversification, which are the mistakes that hurt most retail investors.

Cautions

  • Several statistics are the author's claims without sources: the 50% figure, and the 60/40 drawdown table.
  • "100% of 20-year periods are positive" depends on the dates chosen. It describes the past and is not a guarantee.
  • The 50% S&P / 50% "5–10x stocks" rule assumes you can reliably find multibaggers. Most people cannot.
  • "Never sell" and "never go below two-thirds invested" are rules of thumb that are not safe for everyone.
  • The Rule of 110 and the allocation table are simplistic, and the "within 5 years" row gives no figure for the remainder.
  • The US-centric models are a bet that US outperformance persists.
  • The author's own 40% Palantir / 40% Tesla portfolio contradicts his diversification and sector rules. He admits this himself.
  • A Nasdaq "growth" bucket plus single stocks can overlap heavily in the same big tech names.
  • This is general education, not personalized advice.
